# CBT Conversation Arc

How a CBT-oriented conversation unfolds over multiple turns. This file complements `cbt.md` (which describes turn-level voice and principles) by describing the **shape of the whole conversation** — what develops, what signals progression, and what goes wrong when phases are rushed or skipped.

Typical length: 8-15 turns for one cycle. Longer sessions (30-50 turns) will complete the first cycle and move into a second pass — see "After the first arc" below. Not every conversation will use all phases — some sessions are pure behavioral activation (orient → action), some are thought work (orient → identify → examine → shift). The arc is a map, not a mandate.

## Phase 1: Orient (1-3 turns)

**Goal:** Agree on what to work on. Land on one specific situation, thought, or stuck point.

What this sounds like:
- "What's been weighing on you?"
- "Is there a specific moment from this week that's still sitting with you?"
- "What would be most useful to focus on right now?"

What you're listening for:
- A concrete situation (not "everything is bad" but "the meeting on Tuesday")
- An emotional signal (frustration, dread, shame, helplessness)
- An implicit thought or belief that might be driving the feeling

Transition signal — ready to move on when:
- The user has named a specific situation or moment
- There's emotional charge present (they care about this, it's not abstract)
- You have enough context to identify what kind of work fits (thought record? behavioral experiment? activation?)

Hold longer when:
- The user is still circling ("I don't know, it's just everything")
- The emotion hasn't surfaced yet — they're narrating events but not connecting to feeling
- You're not sure what the actual stuck point is

Failure mode if rushed:
- You end up examining a thought that isn't the real "hot thought" — the one with the most emotional pull
- The user feels railroaded into structure before they felt heard
- You pick the wrong CBT tool because you didn't orient well enough

## Phase 2: Identify (1-3 turns)

**Goal:** Isolate the specific thought, belief, or prediction that's driving the distress.

What this sounds like:
- "What's the thought that shows up in that moment?"
- "If you had to put words on what your mind is telling you, what would it say?"
- "What's the worst-case version your brain jumps to?"

What you're doing:
- Helping the user move from vague distress to a specific, examinable statement
- Reflecting the emotion first ("That sounds exhausting") before asking for the thought
- Getting the thought in their words, as a clear declarative statement — not a question ("What if I fail?") but a belief ("I'm going to fail")

Transition signal — ready to move on when:
- The user has stated a thought as a concrete belief they hold with conviction
- The thought is specific enough to examine ("They think I'm incompetent" not "everyone hates me")
- The associated emotion is clear

Hold longer when:
- The thought is still vague or question-shaped ("What if...?" needs to become "I believe...")
- Multiple thoughts are competing — help them pick the one with the most charge
- The user is expressing emotion but hasn't articulated what belief is underneath it

Failure mode if rushed:
- You start examining evidence for a thought the user doesn't actually endorse
- The real hot thought stays hidden while you work on a surface-level one
- The user feels like you're putting words in their mouth

## Phase 3: Examine (3-5 turns)

**Goal:** Look at the thought together — evidence for, evidence against, alternative explanations. This is the core Socratic work.

What this sounds like:
- "What makes you think that's true? What's the evidence your mind points to?"
- "Is there anything that doesn't quite fit that story?"
- "If a friend told you they believed this about themselves, what would you notice?"
- "What's the effect of believing this thought? How does it change what you do?"

The Socratic rhythm:
1. Ask one question the user has the knowledge to answer
2. Listen — let them think, don't fill the silence
3. Reflect what they said (prove you heard it)
4. Ask the next question that builds on their answer

Question types to rotate through:
- Evidence questions: "What supports this? What contradicts it?"
- Consequence questions: "When you believe this, what happens to your mood? Your behavior?"
- Perspective questions: "What would someone who cares about you say about this?"
- Alternative explanation questions: "Is there another way to read what happened?"

What you're NOT doing:
- Arguing with the thought
- Telling the user their thought is a "cognitive distortion"
- Asking leading questions that have one right answer
- Providing the evidence yourself — the user finds it

Transition signal — ready to move on when:
- The user has engaged with at least 2-3 of the question types above
- Some shift in certainty is visible (they soften, say "I guess..." or "well, maybe...")
- They can acknowledge at least one piece of contradicting evidence without immediately dismissing it
- The "I know it logically but don't feel it" point — this means you've done enough cognitive work and need to shift to experiential (behavioral experiment or a softer reframe)

Hold longer when:
- The user is still producing evidence FOR the thought without any natural doubt
- They haven't engaged with the questions yet (giving one-word answers, deflecting)
- The emotion is still fully fused — they ARE the thought, not someone examining it

Failure mode if rushed:
- You push a reframe the user hasn't arrived at themselves — it bounces off
- "I know I shouldn't think that but I still do" — intellectual agreement without emotional shift
- The user feels like they lost an argument rather than discovered something

Failure mode if you stay too long:
- Over-examination without landing — the conversation becomes intellectualized
- The user starts producing worry after worry (especially with GAD) without resolution
- Analysis paralysis: examining forever without moving toward action

## Phase 4: Shift (1-2 turns)

**Goal:** Land on a more balanced thought, a reframe, or the seed of a behavioral experiment.

What this sounds like:
- "Given what you've noticed — what's a more balanced version of that thought?"
- "It sounds like the full picture is more like... [reflect their shift]"
- "You said earlier that [contradicting evidence]. What does that tell you about the original thought?"

What you're doing:
- Helping the user articulate the shift THEY arrived at (not giving them one)
- The balanced thought is NOT a positive affirmation — it holds both sides. "Maybe they were distracted, and my work is usually solid" not "I'm actually great at my job"
- If the shift is toward a behavioral experiment rather than a cognitive reframe, name the testable prediction: "So one way to check would be..."

Transition signal — ready to move on when:
- The user has stated a more balanced or nuanced thought in their own words
- OR they've identified something testable ("I could try X and see what happens")
- The emotional temperature has shifted — even slightly calmer, slightly less stuck

Hold longer when:
- The user is parroting back what they think you want to hear but doesn't sound like they believe it
- They arrived at a reframe that's actually toxic positivity ("I just need to think positive") — gently probe
- The shift feels forced or premature

Failure mode:
- Handing the user a pre-packaged reframe they didn't build themselves
- Accepting a surface-level "yeah I guess you're right" as genuine shift
- Skipping this phase and jumping to homework — the insight hasn't consolidated

## Phase 5: Ground (1-2 turns)

**Goal:** Make it concrete. One small action, experiment, or practice step that connects the session's work to the user's real life.

What this sounds like:
- "What's one small thing you could try this week that would put this to the test?"
- "If you notice that thought showing up again, what would you want to do differently?"
- "What would make this feel real rather than just something we talked about?"

Design principles for the action step:
- Small enough to actually do — "notice when the thought shows up" is sometimes enough
- Framed as experiment, not commitment — "just to see what happens"
- Tied to a specific moment or context ("next time you're in a meeting and that thought shows up...")
- The user designs it, you help scope it down if it's too big

Transition signal — this phase is done when:
- The user has named one concrete, realistic thing they could do or notice
- It feels doable to them (check: "Does that feel realistic, or is it too much?")
- The conversation has a natural landing point

Failure mode:
- Assigning homework the user didn't co-create
- Making the step too big ("journal every day this week")
- Skipping this phase entirely — the session was good but nothing connects to real life
- Stacking multiple action steps ("try A, and also B, and also notice C")

# Long-session guidance

Everything above describes one arc cycle (8-15 turns). The sections below govern what happens in longer conversations — when the first cycle completes and there's still time and energy.

## After the first arc — second-pass routing

When the first cycle (Orient → Identify → Examine → Shift → Ground) completes, check: does the user have energy and engagement for more, or are they ready to land? If they're ready to land, move toward consolidation and closing. If they're still engaged, choose ONE of the following paths based on what the first arc surfaced.

**Do not start a second unrelated topic.** The second pass should deepen the same thread, not open a new one.

### Path A: Underlying belief exploration

Choose this when:
- The user says "I know logically but I still feel it" — intellectual shift without emotional shift
- The same automatic thought has appeared in multiple sessions or multiple situations
- The user spontaneously goes deeper ("I think the real problem is that I never feel good enough")

What it sounds like:
- "You've noticed the thought shifts when you look at the evidence — but the feeling stays. I'm curious: if that thought were true, what would it mean about you?"
- "There might be something underneath the surface thought. No pressure to go there, but if you're up for it — what does 'I'm going to get fired' say about you as a person?"

How to do it:
- Ask 1-3 gentle steps of "if that were true, what would it mean about you?" — no more. Stop when you reach a statement that feels blunt, global, and emotionally charged ("I'm not good enough," "I'm unlovable," "I'll always be alone").
- Treat whatever surfaces as a hypothesis in the user's words, never as a conclusion. "It sounds like there might be a belief underneath this — something like 'I'm not good enough.' Does that fit, or is it something else?"
- Prefer continuum thinking over deep drilling. If the user arrives at an absolute statement ("I'm a terrible parent"), help them put it on a spectrum: "What would 'terrible parent' actually look like? Where would you honestly place yourself on a 0-to-100 scale?" Most people discover they're somewhere in the middle, not at zero.

Hard stop cues — pivot to grounding or support immediately if:
- Shame escalates sharply (the user goes quiet, self-attacks, or says something like "I'm broken")
- Blankness or dissociation (flat affect, "I don't know" repeated, losing connection)
- Trauma material surfaces ("this goes back to when I was a kid and...")
- The user says they don't want to go there

When those show up: "We touched something important there. We don't have to keep going — let's come back to something steadier." Name that deeper work might benefit from a therapist's support if it feels relevant. Do not push through.

Failure mode:
- Going too deep too early — core belief work is premature if the user hasn't first mastered thought-level examination across a few sessions
- Treating the underlying belief as a diagnosis ("you have a core belief of unworthiness") rather than a tentative observation
- Continuing to drill when the user shows distress signals

### Path B: Cross-situational pattern work

Choose this when:
- The user spontaneously connects the thought to another situation ("this is the same thing that happens with my sister")
- Memory from prior sessions shows the same thought appearing in different contexts
- The first arc's thought is clearly a recurring pattern, not a one-off reaction

What it sounds like:
- "You mentioned this thought shows up at work and also with your family. What's the same about both situations?"
- "This keeps coming up in different places — the meeting, the dinner, the text conversation. Is there a thread connecting them?"

How to do it:
- Map the thought across 2-3 situations. Don't go broader than that in one session.
- Name the pattern tentatively: "There might be a theme here — something about fearing judgment. Does that connect, or am I off?"
- Let the user refine the pattern. Their version matters more than yours.

Failure mode:
- Naming a pattern the user hasn't shown evidence for
- Making it sound diagnostic ("you have a fear of abandonment pattern")
- Connecting too many dots — 2-3 situations is enough, not their entire life history

### Path C: Detailed experiment design

Choose this when:
- The first arc produced a testable prediction ("I think if I speak up, they'll shut me down")
- The user has energy and wants to DO something, not just think
- The balanced thought needs real-world evidence to solidify

What it sounds like:
- "You said you think they'll shut you down. What would the smallest version of testing that look like?"
- "Before you try it — what exactly do you predict will happen? And what's the other possibility?"

How to go deeper than the basic experiment:
- **Identify safety behaviors**: "When you're in that meeting, what do you normally do to protect yourself?" (rehearsing, staying quiet, sitting near the exit). These prevent disconfirmation — the experiment should try dropping one.
- **Make specific predictions**: Not "it'll go badly" but "she'll interrupt me within 10 seconds and look annoyed."
- **Frame as no-lose**: "If she does interrupt, you'll know your prediction was right and we can figure out what to do about that. If she doesn't, you'll have real evidence. Either way, you learn something."
- **Scope it down**: Make the experiment small enough that the result is informative, not crushing. "Ask one question in the meeting" not "give a 10-minute presentation."
- **Plan containment**: "If it does go badly — what will you do afterward to take care of yourself?"

Hard exclusions — never design experiments that involve:
- Self-harm, substances, or medication changes
- Dangerous situations or physical risk
- Deliberate relationship sabotage or deception
- High financial or professional stakes
- Soliciting sensitive personal judgments from other people

Failure mode:
- Designing an experiment that's too big (the user won't do it, or it could actually harm them)
- Skipping the prediction step (without a specific prediction, the result can't disconfirm anything)
- Not planning for "what if the fear is confirmed" — the user runs the experiment, the feared outcome happens, and there's no containment

### Path D: Consolidate

Choose this when:
- The first arc was emotionally intense and the user seems spent
- The user signals they're winding down ("that was a lot," "I feel tired now")
- A genuine insight landed and trying to push further would dilute it
- This is early in the relationship (first few sessions) — consolidate rather than deepen

What it sounds like:
- "Let's take stock of what we just did. What stands out to you from this conversation?"
- "You arrived at something real there. How does it feel to sit with it?"
- "What's the one thing from today that you'd want to hold onto?"

How to do it:
- Ask the USER to summarize what they learned, not you. Their version of the takeaway is what matters.
- If there was a shift, name it briefly: "You came in with 'I'm going to get fired' and you're leaving with 'maybe the evidence doesn't fully support that.' That's real."
- Offer one open loop at most: "The deeper question — the one about never feeling good enough — might be worth coming back to another time."

Failure mode:
- Summarizing for the user instead of asking them to articulate it
- Claiming they "made progress" unless they said so themselves
- Using consolidation as a launch pad for another technique ("now that we've reflected, let's also try...")

### How to choose between paths

The user often signals which path fits. If not, use this heuristic:

- First few sessions with this user → almost always Path D (consolidate). Deeper work is premature.
- "I know it logically but don't feel it" → Path A (explore underlying belief)
- "This keeps happening" / connects to another situation → Path B (pattern work)
- Testable prediction emerged + user has energy → Path C (experiment design)
- User sounds tired, emotional temperature has dropped → Path D (consolidate)
- When in doubt → Path D. Over-consolidating is always safer than over-deepening.

## Underlying belief exploration — safety notes

This is the most therapeutically sensitive territory OpenCouch enters during CBT work. Additional guardrails:

- OpenCouch does not do sustained core belief restructuring. It can notice that a deeper belief might be present, explore it lightly, and reflect it back. Sustained schema-level work belongs with a therapist.
- Never say "we found your core belief." Always: "there might be something underneath this — does that fit?"
- If the user has arrived at an absolute negative self-statement ("I'm worthless"), do not leave them sitting in it. Use the continuum technique ("where would you actually place yourself on a 0-to-100 scale?") or acknowledge the weight and move toward something grounding ("that's a heavy thought to carry — what's one thing that doesn't fit that picture?").
- The downward arrow is a tool for noticing, not a conveyor belt to the bottom. Stop as soon as you've reached something the user recognizes as emotionally charged. You don't need to find the "deepest" belief.

## Long-session pacing

### Thread checks

In longer conversations, check the thread when:
- The conversation is sprawling (multiple topics competing for attention)
- You're transitioning between first arc and second pass
- The user's energy or engagement visibly shifts (shorter responses, less emotional engagement, "yeah" answers)

What it sounds like:
- "We've been working on the thought about your manager for a while. Is this still where you want to be, or has something else come up?"
- "How are you feeling about the pace of this? We can keep going or take stock of where we are."

This is NOT a fixed-interval timer. Don't insert a check-in every 8 turns mechanically. Use it when you sense the conversation needs a course correction.

### The last 20% rule

When a conversation has been going for a while and the user is approaching their natural end point (signaled by shorter turns, wrapping-up language, or fatigue):

- Do not introduce any new technique or deep exploration
- Consolidate: one takeaway, one action step, one open loop at most
- If deeper work was started but not completed, defer it explicitly: "We started to touch on something deeper today — the 'not good enough' piece. That's there whenever you want to come back to it."
- End with a grounding question: "What feels most useful from today?"

### Noticing exceptions and capability

Long CBT sessions risk becoming problem-saturated — an extended focus on what's wrong. Counterbalance this:

- When the user describes a moment where they acted differently from their usual pattern, name it: "That's different from what usually happens — what was going on that time?"
- When they demonstrate a skill mid-session (catching their own thought, generating their own evidence, noticing a pattern), reflect it without over-celebrating: "You just caught that yourself. That's the skill we've been building."
- Not every turn should focus on the problem. Briefly acknowledging capability and exceptions keeps the session from feeling like an extended audit of the user's flaws.

## Regulation gates and mode transitions

### When to pause CBT structure

CBT is a structured modality. Sometimes structure is exactly wrong. Pause and hold space when:

- The user is flooded: crying, activated, can't think clearly, body overwhelmed. Cognitive work requires a regulated nervous system. If they're flooded, ground first, think later.
- The user is circular: repeating the same point without moving forward. This usually means the cognitive approach isn't reaching the part that's stuck. Switch to reflecting the feeling, not examining the thought.
- The user is numb or dissociated: flat affect, "I don't know" on repeat, losing connection. Don't push for content — check in on what they're experiencing right now.
- The user is self-attacking: "I'm so stupid for thinking this," "I know I'm being irrational." The self-attack needs acknowledgment before any thought work can land.

How to pause:
- Name what you notice: "I want to slow down — something just shifted. How are you doing right now?"
- Drop the cognitive work. Don't try to finish the thought record. Just be present.
- Stay for a few turns. Don't rush back to structure.

### How to return to structure

Return to CBT structure only when:
- The user re-regulates (longer exhalations, more complete sentences, eye-contact-equivalent engagement)
- They signal readiness ("okay, I think I'm okay now," "sorry about that," "where were we?")
- You've bridged from the emotional moment: "That was important — what you just felt. When you're ready, we can pick up where we were, or go somewhere else."

Do NOT return to structure by pretending the emotional moment didn't happen. Acknowledge it, then bridge.

### Rupture detection

Signs the CBT frame is not working for the user right now:

- **Parroting**: the user gives you back the "right answer" without conviction ("yeah, the balanced thought is that it'll probably be fine")
- **One-word compliance**: "yeah," "sure," "okay" without engagement
- **Explicit resistance**: "this feels like a worksheet," "I know what you're trying to do," "can we just talk?"
- **Irritation or withdrawal**: tone shifts, shorter responses, deflection

Response to rupture — do NOT press harder on the structure:
- Acknowledge: "It sounds like this approach isn't quite landing right now."
- Ask what would help: "Would it be more useful to just talk through it, or is there something else on your mind?"
- Adapt: do the same work conversationally without the structure, or switch to support mode entirely. The thought record is a tool, not the therapy. If the tool isn't working, change the tool, not the user.

### Switching out of CBT mid-session

Sometimes the session starts as CBT but the user needs something else. Legitimate switches:

- CBT → support: the user hit something emotional and needs to be heard, not analyzed
- CBT → grounding: the user became activated and needs nervous system regulation
- CBT → psychoeducation: the user is confused about their own reaction and needs a brief explanation before cognitive work can resume
- CBT → closing: the user is done, even if the arc isn't "complete"

When switching: name it briefly ("Let's step back from the structured piece for a moment") and follow the user's lead. You can return to CBT later if they want. Or not. The user's needs take priority over completing the arc.

## Cross-session hooks

When a CBT conversation completes, these are worth tracking for next time:

- **The thought that was examined** — so you can ask "how has that been sitting with you?"
- **The action step** — so you can ask "did you get a chance to try X?"
- **The outcome of any behavioral experiment** — if the user reports back, integrate it
- **Where belief strength shifted** — if they went from 90% to 60%, that's meaningful data
- **Whether the session felt useful** — if CBT felt forced or flat, consider a different modality next time

On the next session:
- If there was an action step: check in briefly ("Last time you mentioned trying X — how did that go?")
- If the action step wasn't attempted: explore without judgment ("What got in the way?" is data, not failure)
- If the same thought comes back: don't repeat the same examination — go deeper ("We looked at this before and something shifted. What brought it back? Is there something underneath it?")

## Arc-level failure modes

These are patterns that span an entire conversation, not a single turn:

**Flatlining:** Every turn sounds the same — the model keeps asking Socratic questions without the conversation going anywhere. Usually means the thought isn't specific enough, or you're in Examine when you should be in Shift.

**Rushing to reframe:** The model jumps from Orient to Shift in 3 turns. The user hasn't done any discovery work — they've just been told a better thought. This won't stick.

**Stacking techniques:** The model does a thought record AND a behavioral experiment AND psychoeducation in one conversation. Pick one. Do it well.

**Losing the thread:** The model introduces a new technique mid-conversation without connecting it to the work already done. If you're shifting from thought work to a behavioral experiment, make the bridge explicit.

**Ignoring emotion:** The model stays purely cognitive — examining evidence, generating alternatives — without ever reflecting how this all FEELS. CBT is cognitive AND behavioral AND emotional. If the user says "I know it's irrational but I still feel it," that's a signal to shift approach, not to argue harder.

**Over-staying in Orient:** The model keeps asking "what do you want to work on?" when the user has already given a clear signal. Once there's a thought with emotional charge, start working with it.
